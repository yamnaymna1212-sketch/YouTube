And the party that has to fix it just lost its majority.

On November third, voters handed Congress to the Democrats. Jake doesn't care who won. He cares about one question. What does this do to my money?

Because Congress decides three things that hit your wallet. Whether the government stays open. What some things cost at the store. And who pays for the war.

Three doors. Let's open them, in the order they hit.

First, a reality check. Nothing changes tomorrow. The new Congress doesn't take office until January third. Until then, the old one is still in charge. And the president is still the president, with a veto pen.

Here's how it works, in sixty seconds. For anything to become law, three things have to happen. The House passes it. The Senate passes it. And the president signs it. If the president says no, a veto, then it takes two-thirds of both chambers to override. That almost never happens.

So what did Democrats actually win? Mostly, one thing. The power of the purse. The Constitution says no money leaves the Treasury unless Congress passes a law for it. Every dollar. Which means that from January, no spending bill passes without them. And the president can't spend what Congress won't fund.

That's why the first date matters.

Door one. The shutdown clock.

Right now, the government is running on a stopgap bill. Congress passed it in September, with big bipartisan votes. It keeps spending about where it was last year. And it expires on December eleventh.

If Congress doesn't pass a new bill, or another stopgap, by then, funding lapses. Parts of the government close.

You've seen what that looks like. Last year, the government shut down for forty-three days. The longest in American history.

A shutdown doesn't stop everything. The military keeps working. Air traffic controllers keep working. Social Security and Medicare payments keep going out. But many of those people are working without pay, and the offices that serve the public slow down or close. National parks. Some loan and permit offices. The longer it lasts, the more it spreads.

Meet Dana, Jake's cousin. She's an illustration, not a real person, but her story is real for hundreds of thousands of people. She works for the federal government. During a shutdown, she keeps showing up, or she stays home, and either way, the paycheck stops.

The Congressional Budget Office estimated that furloughed federal workers would be owed about four hundred million dollars in back pay for every day it lasted. Federal employees do get paid back later. Contractors generally don't. And the CBO said at least seven billion dollars of output was lost for good.

Now here's the part people miss. The people who decide this by December eleventh aren't the new Congress. They're the old one. The party that lost still holds both chambers until January third. One senior House Republican has already predicted another stopgap, pushing the fight into the new year.

And we've seen this before. In twenty-eighteen, after Democrats won the House, the government shut down in December and stayed shut for about five weeks, into the new Congress.

So what could happen to you? One. Another stopgap, and nothing changes for now. Two. A full-year deal. Three. A shutdown. And if it happens, the first thing you'd notice isn't a headline. It's a delay. A paycheck. A permit. A park gate. An airport line.

Jake checks his own bills. Rent. Phone. Car payment. None of them come from the government. But the second door is the one he's already standing in.

Door two. The tariff bill.

Quick history. On February twentieth this year, the Supreme Court ruled six to three that the president can't impose tariffs using an emergency law called IEEPA. The case was Learning Resources versus Trump.

The Court didn't say all tariffs are illegal. It said this tool, in this way, wasn't allowed. Tariffs under other laws, like Section two thirty-two, are still in place. And the authority to tax has to come from Congress, or from a law that clearly hands it to the president.

First, how a tariff actually reaches you. Say a shop imports a part that costs a hundred dollars, and the tariff is ten percent. This is just an example. The shop pays ten dollars to the government, at the border. Then the shop has choices. Eat the cost. Raise its price. Or find another supplier. Which one it picks depends on the shop, the product, and the competition. That's why one tariff can show up in your bill in some places and not at all in others.

Now, people disagree about tariffs, and it's worth hearing both sides. Supporters say they protect American jobs, bring other countries to the table, and raise money for the Treasury. Critics say they work like a tax, because the importer pays at the border and the cost tends to travel down to the shelf. Economists argue about how much, and on which products. That argument isn't settled, and we won't pretend it is.

Then came the refunds. Companies that paid those tariffs were owed money back. By early October, customs numbers pointed to more than a hundred billion dollars already returned, including interest. Sources differ on the exact figure, so treat it as a ballpark.

Here's the catch for you. Those refunds go to the importers. The businesses that paid at the border. Not to you. If a store raised its prices because of those tariffs, nothing in the ruling sends that money back to your wallet.

So what does the new Congress change? Less than you'd think. It can hold hearings. It can demand documents. It can write a tariff bill. But if the president vetoes it, it takes two-thirds of both chambers to override.

So what could happen to your money? One. The remaining tariffs stay, and prices stay where they are. Two. A trade deal changes them. Three. New tariffs under other laws. Which is why the number to watch isn't a headline. It's the price tag on the shelf.

Door three. The one nobody puts on a thumbnail. The bill for the war.

You remember Jake's bunker from the last video. The war with Iran began on February twenty-eighth, and Congress never voted to authorize it. By May, the Pentagon estimated it had cost about twenty-nine billion dollars.

That money has to come from somewhere. From other parts of the budget, or from borrowing. And borrowing has a price: interest, paid out of the same federal budget as everything else.

Now the power of the purse comes back. In January, Democrats can decide whether to fund more of the war, to attach conditions to it, or to say no. Republicans can argue the opposite. And both sides have a point. One says funding the troops is non-negotiable. The other says Congress should vote before billions are spent.

And remember how borrowing works. Every dollar the government borrows is paid back with interest, and that interest is a bill that comes due in every future budget. So a decision made in January keeps showing up for years.

Whichever way it goes, the question is the same. Who pays? Higher prices at the pump when oil spikes. Higher taxes someday. Or less spending somewhere else. Oil has already swung more than ten dollars a barrel in a single week this month.

Now let's make it personal. Four people. Three doors.

Maya rents an apartment and drives to work. Door one barely touches her, unless a long shutdown slows the economy. Door two is hers, the price tag on the shelf. And door three reaches her at the pump.

Walter is retired and lives on Social Security. In past shutdowns, those checks have kept going out, because they're paid through a separate system. What can slow down is service, the phone line, the office visit. His real risk is quieter: if prices stay high, a fixed check buys less.

Dana, the federal worker, lives in door one. In a shutdown she's furloughed, or working without pay. She's guaranteed back pay later. But rent is due now. For her, a cash cushion matters most.

And Luis owns a small shop that imports parts. Door two is his. If he paid those tariffs, he may be owed a refund. But it doesn't arrive on its own. He has to file a claim.

Four people, one Congress, four very different months.

Let's put it on one calendar. December eleventh: the stopgap runs out. January third: the new Congress takes office. After that, the real budget fight begins. Those are the two dates that matter. Everything in between is noise, and noise is where headlines live.
