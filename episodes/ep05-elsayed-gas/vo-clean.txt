Right now, regular gas averages four dollars and fifteen cents nationwide. That's a dollar thirteen more than a year ago. In Jake's Midwest, four-ten. On the West Coast, almost five-thirty.

Here's what's inside that price. According to the Energy Information Administration, last year about half of every gallon was crude oil. About a sixth was refining. About a sixth was getting it to the station. And about a sixth was taxes.

That first half is the part that moves. And crude oil is priced on a world market.

A barrel holds forty-two gallons. So every time oil goes up one dollar a barrel, gas tends to go up about two and a half cents a gallon. Ten dollars on a barrel, about a quarter at the pump.

And this year, oil didn't move ten dollars. It moved a lot more. Because of one narrow strip of water.

The Strait of Hormuz. Normally, about a fifth of the world's oil passes through it. When the war started in February, traffic through the strait collapsed. Oil spiked. And in September, in Michigan, Jake's tank hit seventy-five dollars.

So keep this picture in your head. Most of your gas price is decided far away from the White House. Now, here's what a president can still do about it.

Lever one. The emergency tank.

The government keeps oil in giant salt caverns along the Gulf Coast. It's called the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, and a president can order it released, fast.

This year, that already happened. In March, the administration ordered one hundred seventy-two million barrels released, as part of a four-hundred-million-barrel release with other countries.

It helped cushion the spike. But it has a cost. By August, the reserve fell below three hundred million barrels. The lowest since nineteen eighty-three.

So a President El-Sayed would inherit a tank that's much emptier than it used to be. And the government plans to buy oil back to refill it. Buying oil back means more demand, at some point, on the same market.

Lever one is real. But it's a cushion, not a cure. And once you use it, you have to refill it.

Lever two. The one politicians love. The gas tax.

The federal gas tax is eighteen point four cents a gallon. It hasn't changed since nineteen ninety-three.

Both El-Sayed and Rogers backed pausing it. So would it happen?

Here's the catch. A president can't pause it alone. Only Congress can. And Congress has never actually done it. Bills to suspend it were introduced this year, and went nowhere.

Why? Because that money pays for highways and bridges. Pause it, and the Highway Trust Fund loses billions that have to come from somewhere else.

And would the full eighteen cents even reach you? Economists disagree. Some of it could stay with sellers, especially when supply is tight.

Let's do Jake's math anyway. He buys about fifteen gallons a week. That's seven hundred and eighty a year. A full year with no federal gas tax, if every cent reached him, saves him about a hundred and forty dollars.

Real money. Remember that number.

Lever three. The small switches.

In an emergency, a president's agencies can waive some fuel rules. Allow more ethanol in summer gas. Or let foreign ships carry fuel between American ports, by waiving a century-old shipping law called the Jones Act.

This year, the administration used both.

Estimates put the effect at a few cents a gallon. Helpful. But you'd barely see it on the pump sign.

Lever four. The big one. And it isn't in any energy plan.

Remember the strait. This year's spike didn't come from a tax or a regulation. It came from a war.

And on war and diplomacy, a president has more power than on almost anything else. Whether to escalate. Whether to negotiate. Whether a shipping lane opens, or stays closed.

Here's the math. If oil drops twenty dollars a barrel, gas tends to fall around fifty cents. For Jake, that's about three hundred and seventy dollars a year. More than two and a half times the gas tax.

And it works in both directions. A new crisis could add a dollar to every gallon. That's seven hundred and eighty dollars a year, for Jake alone.

El-Sayed has called the Iran war illegal and blamed it for the spike. Supporters of the war say it was necessary for security, and that prices will come down once it's over. Either way, the lesson is the same. The biggest gas decision a president makes might not look like a gas decision at all.

Lever five. The slow one.

This is where the two sides really split. One side says drill more. Open more federal land. Build more pipelines. More supply, lower prices. The other side says get off oil. More renewables, more electric cars, so the next war in the Gulf doesn't hit your wallet so hard.

El-Sayed is clearly on the second side. Rogers argued for all of the above.

But here's what both sides tend to leave out. These policies take years. A new oil lease can take years to produce a single barrel. A new car takes years to replace an old one. And the United States is already the largest oil producer in the world, and prices still spiked this year, because oil is priced globally.

So lever five matters. For your kids' gas bill. Not for next week's.
