Sunday, six p.m. Eastern. Nothing has happened to you yet. But in this what-if, somewhere in the Gulf, a nuclear weapon has just been used. The banks are closed. Wall Street is closed. And the first markets on Earth are about to open. What they do in the next twenty-four hours decides what your gas, your savings, and your retirement account look like on Monday morning. This is the first day. Hour by hour.

First, what this is. It is a what-if. Nobody can know how markets react to something that has never happened, so we will not guess a number. Instead, we will use real days from real crises as yardsticks. September eleventh. A Saudi oil plant in twenty nineteen. A bank run in twenty twenty-three. A Sunday night in March, twenty twenty. Each one broke one part of the system. This what-if would test all of them in a single day.

Three doors. Your stocks. Your gas. Your bank. And one clock that never stops. Let's start it.

Meet Jake. Twenty-six. He drives to work, fifteen gallons to a tank, and he has a small retirement account he never checks. It's Sunday night. His phone just lit up. And he has three questions. What happens to the market? What happens at the pump? And is my money safe?

Door one. Stocks. Hour zero to hour six. Here is the first surprise. The stock market isn't fully dark on a Sunday night. Futures, which are bets on where the market will open, trade into the evening, and Asian exchanges open while America sleeps. So the first price anyone sees is a bet, made by a few traders, with the whole world watching.

But there is a safety switch, and it is written down in advance. During regular trading hours, if the S and P five hundred falls seven percent from yesterday's close, trading stops everywhere for fifteen minutes. At thirteen percent, it stops again for fifteen minutes. At twenty percent, trading is over for the day. The first two only apply before three twenty-five in the afternoon, and each can happen only once a day.

Individual stocks have their own pauses too. If one stock moves too far, too fast, within five minutes, it freezes on its own. Those bands run from five to twenty percent, depending on the stock. So the system doesn't run out of brakes. It runs out of patience.

Is that normal? Not usually. But in March of twenty twenty, the first level triggered four times in a matter of days.

Our yardstick is September eleventh, two thousand one. Wall Street closed for four days, the longest shutdown since the nineteen thirties. When it reopened, the Dow fell six hundred eighty-four points in one day. More than seven percent. At the time, the biggest one-day point drop in its history.

For Jake, a seven percent drop on a ten thousand dollar account is seven hundred dollars. On paper. It only becomes real if he sells. So the honest answer to door one is this. Nobody knows the number. What we know is the machinery, and that it was built before the panic.

Door two. Oil and the pump. Hour six to hour twelve. About twenty million barrels of oil move through the Strait of Hormuz every day. Roughly a fifth of everything the world burns. In this what-if, the Gulf is the headline. So every oil trader, every tanker, every shipping desk asks the same question at the same moment. Do we still go?

Most of that oil heads to Asia. So why would an American driver care? Because oil is priced on one world market. A barrel costs what the world is willing to pay for it, and crude is more than half of what you pay at the pump.

Our yardstick: a Saturday in September, twenty nineteen. Drones hit Saudi Arabia's biggest oil processing plant and knocked out five point seven million barrels a day. On Monday, Brent crude closed up fourteen point six percent. Its biggest one-day percentage gain since at least nineteen eighty-eight. That was one plant. This what-if is a whole region.

Now, the pump. Here is the rule of thumb. One dollar on a barrel moves gas about two point four cents a gallon. Ten dollars, about twenty-four cents. On Jake's fifteen gallon tank, that is three dollars sixty. Twenty dollars on a barrel, seven dollars twenty. His tank goes from sixty-two thirty to about sixty-nine fifty.

But not tonight. Gas climbs like a rocket and falls like a feather. Studies find that pump prices rise faster than they fall. So the first week is the expensive one, and the recovery is slow.

And the cushion? Last March, the government released a hundred seventy-two million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. By August the reserve had fallen below three hundred million barrels, the lowest since nineteen eighty-three. So the cushion is thinner than it used to be.

Door three. Your bank. Hour twelve to hour twenty-four. This one moves the fastest. On March ninth, twenty twenty-three, depositors tried to pull forty-two billion dollars out of Silicon Valley Bank in eight hours. About a quarter of everything the bank held. California regulators said social media and phone banking made the run faster than any before it. By the next morning, the bank was closed.

So what protects you? Deposit insurance. Two hundred fifty thousand dollars per person, per bank, per ownership category. It covers checking, savings, money market accounts and C Ds. It does not cover stocks or bonds.

And Washington has done Sunday nights before. On Sunday, March fifteenth, twenty twenty, the Federal Reserve cut its rate to near zero and promised at least seven hundred billion dollars in bond purchases, before the market opened Monday. An emergency Sunday move isn't science fiction. It's already in the playbook.

Now let's meet three illustrative people. They are not real. They are here to make the math human. Maya drives forty miles each way to work. She buys almost twice as much gas as Jake, so every swing hits her twice. Luis drives a diesel delivery van. Diesel is already over six dollars a gallon, and that cost travels from his tank to the grocery shelf. And Walter is retired. He barely drives. For him, it shows up in the grocery bill, and in a statement he opens once a quarter.

So here is the whole day, on one clock. Sunday, six p.m. Futures and Asian markets open. Monday, nine thirty a.m. Wall Street opens, and the circuit breakers are armed. All day Monday, oil trades, tankers move, and banks open their doors. Monday, six p.m. The first full day is over. And what you saw at hour zero looks very different at hour twenty-four. Panic is fast. Prices are slow.

Three things worth knowing before any day like this. One. Know what is insured. Two hundred fifty thousand dollars, per person, per bank, per category. Two. Know your time horizon. Retirement money is long money, and a paper loss isn't a loss until you sell. Three. Watch the pump over weeks, not hours. Rockets up, feathers down.

Back to Sunday, six p.m. Nothing has happened. This is a what-if. But the clock is real, the rules are real, and so are the yardsticks. Each of those days tested one piece of the system. Nobody has ever tested all the pieces on the same day. That is why this is a what-if, and why it is worth knowing the rules.
