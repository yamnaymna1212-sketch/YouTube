Sunday, six p.m. Eastern. Nothing has happened to you yet. But in this what-if, in a port city on the Gulf, a nuclear weapon has just been used. Two people are about to spend the next twenty-four hours very differently. One has minutes to find a safe place. The other has a phone, a TV, and a gas tank. This is the first day. Hour by hour. And we'll follow the money, and the one thing that keeps people safe.

First, what this is. It is a what-if. Nobody can know exactly what would happen, so we will not guess numbers. We will use two kinds of real information. Official safety guidance, from the people whose job it is to write it. And real days from real crises, as yardsticks. September eleventh. A Saudi oil plant in twenty nineteen. A bank run in twenty twenty-three. A Sunday night in March, twenty twenty. We will not show anything graphic. This is about decisions and prices.

Meet Sam. Illustrative. Not a real person. Sam is a delivery driver in the port city, and Sam's phone just buzzed with an alert. And meet Jake, twenty-six, thousands of miles away, fifteen gallons to a tank, a small retirement account he never checks. Two screens. One clock.

Hour zero. Sam's first minutes. Official guidance, from the U.S. government's own pages, is three short sentences. Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned. That is the whole plan. Get inside the nearest solid building, right away. A car does not count as shelter. Neither does the open street. You may have only minutes.

Where inside? Go to the middle of the building, or a basement, away from doors and windows. The more walls and the more distance between you and the outside, the better. A tall concrete building beats a thin wooden one.

You might ask, what about a tunnel, or a subway? Honest answer: official guidance names basements and the middle of big buildings. It does not give a rule for tunnels, so we will not invent one. Whatever the place, the rule is the same. Get inside, and follow your local officials.

So Sam walks into the nearest concrete building, takes the stairs to the middle floor, sits in an inner hallway, and does the hardest part. Nothing. Sam stays put. And stays tuned: a battery radio, a phone on low power, the official channels. Officials, not individuals, decide when it is safe to come out.

Now Jake. Hour zero to hour six. Door one. Stocks. Sunday night, the stock market isn't fully dark. Futures, which are bets on where the market will open, trade into the evening, and Asian exchanges open while America sleeps. So the first price Jake sees on TV is a bet, made by a few traders, with the whole world watching.

But there is a safety switch, and it is written down in advance. During regular trading hours, if the S and P five hundred falls seven percent from yesterday's close, trading stops everywhere for fifteen minutes. At thirteen percent, again for fifteen minutes. At twenty percent, trading is over for the day. The first two only apply before three twenty-five in the afternoon, and each can happen only once a day. Individual stocks have their own pauses, too, if they move too far, too fast.

Our yardstick is September eleventh, two thousand one. Wall Street closed for four days, the longest shutdown since the nineteen thirties. When it reopened, the Dow fell six hundred eighty-four points in one day. More than seven percent. At the time, the biggest one-day point drop in its history.

For Jake, a seven percent drop on a ten thousand dollar account is seven hundred dollars. On paper. It only becomes real if he sells. Nobody knows the number tonight. What we know is the machinery, and that it was built before the panic.

Back to Sam. Hour six. The phone says seventeen percent. Sam turns the screen brightness down, and keeps one thing on: the official channel. If Sam came in from outside, there is one more step the guidance gives. Take off the outer clothes and seal them in a bag. That removes much of the dust that can carry radiation. Then wash with soap and water. Don't scrub hard. Don't panic. Waiting is the job.

Now Jake. Door two. Oil and the pump. Hour six to hour twelve. About twenty million barrels of oil move through the Strait of Hormuz every day. Roughly a fifth of everything the world burns. In this what-if, the Gulf is the headline. So every oil trader, every tanker, every shipping desk asks one question at the same moment. Do we still go?

Most of that oil heads to Asia. So why would an American driver care? Because oil is priced on one world market. A barrel costs what the world is willing to pay for it, and crude is more than half of what you pay at the pump.

Our yardstick: a Saturday in September, twenty nineteen. Drones hit Saudi Arabia's biggest oil processing plant and knocked out five point seven million barrels a day. On Monday, Brent crude closed up fourteen point six percent. Its biggest one-day percentage gain since at least nineteen eighty-eight. That was one plant. This what-if is a whole region.

Now the pump. Rule of thumb: one dollar on a barrel moves gas about two point four cents a gallon. Ten dollars, about twenty-four cents. On Jake's fifteen gallon tank, that is three dollars sixty. Twenty dollars on a barrel, seven dollars twenty. His tank goes from sixty-two thirty to about sixty-nine fifty. But not tonight. Gas climbs like a rocket and falls like a feather. So the first week is the expensive one, and the recovery is slow.

And the cushion? Last March, the government released a hundred seventy-two million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. By August the reserve had fallen below three hundred million barrels, the lowest since nineteen eighty-three. The cushion is thinner than it used to be.

Hour twelve. Sam hears a short official message. Stay where you are. Another update soon. That is it. No drama. And that is the point. The people who get through the first day are mostly the ones who follow one boring instruction.

Jake, hour twelve to hour twenty-four. Door three. His bank. This one moves the fastest. On March ninth, twenty twenty-three, depositors tried to pull forty-two billion dollars out of Silicon Valley Bank in eight hours. About a quarter of everything the bank held. California regulators said social media and phone banking made it faster than any run before. By the next morning, the bank was closed.

So what protects you? Deposit insurance. Two hundred fifty thousand dollars per person, per bank, per ownership category. It covers checking, savings, money market accounts and C Ds. It does not cover stocks or bonds.

And Washington has done Sunday nights before. On Sunday, March fifteenth, twenty twenty, the Federal Reserve cut its rate to near zero and promised at least seven hundred billion dollars in bond purchases, before the market opened Monday. An emergency Sunday move isn't science fiction. It's in the playbook.

Daily life changes too, and not only at the pump. On September eleventh, the entire U.S. airspace was closed, and flights stayed grounded for days. A what-if like this would test supply chains, flights, shipping routes, all at once. Maya drives forty miles each way to work, so she feels the pump twice as hard as Jake. Luis drives a diesel delivery van, and diesel is already over six dollars a gallon, so his cost travels to the grocery shelf. Walter is retired and barely drives; he feels it in the grocery bill. All three are illustrative, here to make the math human.

Hour twenty-four. Sam is still inside, still tuned in. The update is the same, and it is the best news there could be. Stay. Because officials, not individuals, decide when it's safe, and sometimes that is more than one day. Jake's day ends the other way. Gas is higher. The market is rattled. But his bank is open. His insured money is insured. And his retirement account is still a number on a screen.

So here is the whole day, on one clock. Sunday six p.m. Futures and Asian markets open. Monday nine thirty a.m. Wall Street opens and the circuit breakers are armed. All day Monday, oil trades, tankers move, banks open their doors. Monday six p.m. The first full day is over. Panic is fast. Prices are slow.

Three things worth knowing. One. If you ever hear an alert like this, the official guidance is three sentences. Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Two. Know what's insured: two hundred fifty thousand dollars, per person, per bank, per category. Three. A paper loss isn't a loss until you sell, and the pump moves over weeks, not hours.

Back to Sunday, six p.m. Nothing has happened. This is a what-if. But the guidance is real, the rules are real, and so are the yardsticks. Each of those days tested one piece of the system. Nobody has ever tested all the pieces on the same day. That is why this is a what-if, and why it's worth knowing the rules.
