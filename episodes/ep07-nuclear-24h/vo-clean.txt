Sunday, six p.m. Eastern. Nothing has happened to you yet. But in this what-if, in a port city on the Gulf, a nuclear weapon has just been used. Two people are about to spend the next twenty-four hours very differently. One has minutes to find a safe place. The other has a phone, a TV, and a gas tank. This is the first day. Hour by hour.

First, what this is. A what-if. Nobody can know exactly what would happen, so we won't guess numbers. We'll use official safety guidance, and real days from real crises as yardsticks. Nothing graphic. This is about decisions, and prices.

Meet Sam. Illustrative, not a real person. A delivery driver in the port city. And meet Jake, twenty-six, thousands of miles away, fifteen gallons to a tank. Two screens. One clock.

Hour zero. Sam's phone buzzes. The official guidance, from U.S. government pages, is three short sentences. Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned. Get inside the nearest solid building, right away. A car doesn't count as shelter. Neither does the street. You may have only minutes.

Go to the middle of the building, or a basement, away from doors and windows. What about a tunnel, or a subway? Official guidance gives no rule for those, so we won't invent one. Sam climbs to the middle floor, sits in an inner hallway, and does the hardest part. Nothing. Sam stays put, stays tuned, and waits. Officials, not individuals, decide when it's safe.

Now Jake. Hour zero to six. Door one. Stocks. On a Sunday night the market isn't fully dark. Futures, bets on where it will open, trade into the evening, and Asian exchanges open while America sleeps. So the first price Jake sees is a bet, made by a few traders, with the world watching.

But there's a safety switch, written down in advance. If the S and P five hundred falls seven percent in a day, trading stops for fifteen minutes. At thirteen percent, again. At twenty percent, trading is over for the day.

Our yardstick: September eleventh, two thousand one. Wall Street closed for four days. When it reopened, the Dow fell six hundred eighty-four points in one day. More than seven percent. On a ten thousand dollar account, that's seven hundred dollars. On paper. It only becomes real if you sell.

Door two. Oil and the pump. Hour six to twelve. About twenty million barrels of oil move through the Strait of Hormuz every day. Roughly a fifth of everything the world burns. Oil has one world price, so a shock in the Gulf reaches an American pump.

Our yardstick: September twenty nineteen. Drones hit Saudi Arabia's biggest oil plant. On Monday, Brent crude closed up fourteen point six percent, its biggest one-day gain since at least nineteen eighty-eight. That was one plant. This what-if is a whole region.

The rule of thumb: one dollar on a barrel moves gas about two point four cents a gallon. Twenty dollars, about forty-eight cents. On Jake's fifteen gallons, seven dollars twenty. His tank goes from sixty-two thirty to about sixty-nine fifty. Not tonight. Gas climbs like a rocket and falls like a feather.

Hour twelve. Sam hears one short official message. Stay where you are. No drama. That's the point. Most people get through the first day by following one boring instruction.

Door three. His bank. Hour twelve to twenty-four. This one moves the fastest. On March ninth, twenty twenty-three, depositors tried to pull forty-two billion dollars out of Silicon Valley Bank in eight hours. About a quarter of everything it held. Deposit insurance covers two hundred fifty thousand dollars, per person, per bank, per category. Checking, savings, C Ds. Not stocks or bonds.

Daily life changes too. Shipping routes, supply chains, grocery prices. Maya drives forty miles each way, so she feels the pump twice as hard. Luis drives a diesel van, and his cost travels to the grocery shelf. Both are illustrative.

Hour twenty-four. Sam is still inside, still tuned in. The update is the best news there could be. Stay. Jake's day ends the other way. Gas is higher. The market is rattled. But his bank is open, and his insured money is insured.

Three things worth knowing. One. The official guidance is three sentences. Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Two. Know what's insured. Three. A paper loss isn't a loss until you sell, and the pump moves over weeks, not hours.

Back to Sunday, six p.m. Nothing has happened. This is a what-if. But the guidance is real, the rules are real, and so are the yardsticks. Panic is fast. Prices are slow.
