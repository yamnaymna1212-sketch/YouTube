Seven fifteen. Health care. Jake checks his pay stub. A typical family health plan costs about twenty-six thousand nine hundred ninety-three dollars a year. The worker pays sixty-eight fifty of that. The employer pays the rest, and it never shows up on the paycheck. For a single worker like Jake, the share is about fourteen hundred forty dollars a year.

El-Sayed's big idea is Medicare for All. One public plan, instead of plans through your employer. If it passed, that line on Jake's stub could disappear. Insurance premiums could go away. But nothing is free. The money would have to come from somewhere else, most likely taxes. So Jake's stub could lose one line, and gain another.

Outside studies that tried to price it land between about thirty-two and thirty-four trillion dollars over ten years. Supporters say it replaces money we already spend, and cuts insurance overhead. Critics say it would mean much higher taxes. The honest answer is that the design decides. And a president can't pass it alone. Congress has to.

Eight a.m. The pump. Regular is about four dollars fifteen a gallon. Jake fills fifteen gallons. Sixty-two dollars thirty. Over a year of driving, that's about a hundred forty dollars for Jake. But only Congress can pause it. A president can ask. He can't sign it into existence. What a president can do alone is smaller. Last March, the administration released a hundred seventy-two million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. That's a real lever. It helps for weeks, not for years.

El-Sayed also backs a fast shift to clean energy. His opponent says that would raise energy costs. Fact-checkers found he never proposed banning gas cars. Over years, it could change what Jake drives. It doesn't change the price tonight.

Noon. Groceries. What you pay at the register is shaped by tariffs, shipping, wages, and how much competition a store faces. El-Sayed talks about stronger unions, and about enforcing rules against big monopolies. A president can direct agencies that do that work. It is slow, and it happens case by case. Tariffs are another piece. In February, the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs the White House had used. Who pays for tariffs, and which ones stay, is a fight between the president, Congress, and the courts.

Six p.m. Taxes and rent. El-Sayed has proposed higher taxes on billionaires, lifting the payroll tax cap for high earners, and ending the tax on Social Security benefits for seniors. Supporters say that money would pay for services. Critics say higher taxes on the rich can slow investment and hiring. Either way, most of it wouldn't touch Jake's paycheck directly. And rent? Rules on rent are mostly set by states and cities. A president can't cap Jake's rent.

Before Jake adds it up, the three keys. A big law needs three yeses. The House. The Senate, where most bills need sixty votes to get past a filibuster. And the president's signature. A president holds one of the three. What he can do alone is smaller: direct agencies, appoint officials, issue orders that a court can still block, and veto a bill he dislikes.

Nine p.m. Jake adds it all up. Health care: a big change, if Congress passes it. Gas: small, and slow. Taxes: it depends on the bill. Groceries: slow. The pattern is the same one we keep finding. A president sets the direction. Congress, the courts, and the world set most of the numbers.

So what was Jake's life after El-Sayed became president? On day one, almost the same as before. The change comes through laws, and laws take votes.
