Imagine. Seven a.m. Abdul El-Sayed is president, and everything he campaigned on works. Every bill passes. Every promise lands. Jake, twenty-six, wakes up in the best case. Today we follow one dream day, hour by hour. And at the end, we'll check how much of it is real.

First, what this is. A what-if, the best-case version. On November third, Michigan elected Abdul El-Sayed to the Senate. President is a bigger job, and years away. Every scene that starts with the word imagine is a dream. The numbers are real arithmetic. The outcomes are not promises. Supporters and critics both get their say.

Meet Jake. Illustrative, not a real person. Single, fifteen gallons to a tank, health plan through his employer. Let's wake him up.

Imagine, seven fifteen. Jake opens his pay stub. The health insurance line is gone. Medicare for All passed. Right now, a single worker's share is about fourteen hundred and forty dollars a year. In this dream, that stays in his pocket. Fewer bills at the doctor. No new plan every time he changes jobs. No open enrollment forms. He walks into a clinic with one card, and that's it.

Reality check one. Outside studies price a plan like this at thirty-two to thirty-four trillion dollars over ten years. Supporters say it replaces money we already spend. Critics say taxes would rise a lot. And Congress has to pass it. In the dream, the stub loses a line. In real life, it may gain another one, a tax.

Imagine, eight a.m. Peace in the Gulf. The war with Iran is over. El-Sayed has called that war illegal. Oil falls twenty dollars a barrel. Here's the arithmetic. One dollar on a barrel moves gas about two point four cents a gallon. Twenty dollars, about forty-eight cents. Over a year of driving, that's about three hundred seventy dollars for Jake. Picture the pump at the station on his corner, ticking slower than it did last week.

Now push the dream further. Imagine gas falls a whole dollar sixty-five, to about two fifty a gallon. Jake's tank drops from sixty-two thirty to about thirty-seven fifty. Over a year, about twelve hundred eighty-seven dollars. That is not a promise. Nobody has promised that. It's just math on a bigger dream. Picture him filling up, and the total stopping at thirty-seven fifty. That's the feeling the dream sells.

Reality check two. A president doesn't set the price of gas. The world oil market does. And a president can end a war only with Congress, because the Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war. Peace can lower oil. It doesn't set the price at the pump.

Imagine, noon. Jake's grocery receipt is shorter. He checks it twice. Big monopolies broken up. Unions stronger, wages higher. Be fair about this one. What you pay at the store depends on tariffs, trucks, wages, and competition. A president can push on some of those. In February, the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs. Nothing here moves in a day.

Imagine, six p.m. Taxes. Billionaires pay more. The payroll tax cap for high earners is lifted. Seniors stop paying tax on Social Security. Jake's grandmother opens her monthly check, and it's a little bigger. Supporters say that money pays for services. Critics say higher taxes on the rich can slow investment and hiring. Either way, most of it wouldn't touch Jake's paycheck directly. And rent? Mostly state and city law. A president can't cap rent.

Every dream hits the same wall. Before it ends, the three keys. A big law needs three yeses. The House. The Senate, where most bills need sixty votes. And the president's signature. A president holds one of the three.

Nine p.m. Jake adds up the best case. Health care, fourteen hundred forty dollars, if Congress passes it, and maybe a new tax. Gas, twelve eighty-seven, though nobody promised it. The war piece is already inside the gas number. Add it up, and the dream is worth about twenty-seven hundred dollars a year. Real money. But not half off everything. About two hundred twenty-five dollars a month. Enough to notice. Not enough to quit your job.

Imagine, ten p.m. Jake turns off the light. The news is quiet. No sirens, no breaking alerts. Then the alarm rings. It's Monday. And the Senate hasn't voted yet.

That's the honest size of the dream. Real, and worth talking about. And every dollar of it needs votes. Which is why the real story isn't a president. It's the people who vote on every one of these bills.
