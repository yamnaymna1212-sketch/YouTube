Donald Trump cannot run in 2028. The Constitution says two terms. Period. So whoever wins on November seventh, 2028, inherits today's prices, today's tariffs, and today's interest rates. Here's what the next president really walks into, and what it means for your money.

This isn't a prediction about who wins. Republican, Democrat, someone new. It doesn't matter for this video. The bills are already on the table. And the next president starts paying them on January twentieth, 2029.

Here's the thing most people miss. Presidents get the credit, or the blame, for prices they didn't start. The economy moves slowly. What you pay in 2029 is being decided right now, in courtrooms, in Congress, and at the Federal Reserve.

Let's start with the receipt. Yesterday, the government's inflation report came out. Prices rose four tenths of a percent in October. In one month. Groceries, up. Rent, up. Beef, up almost one percent. That might sound small. But it stacks, month after month, on top of everything you already pay.

Door number one. Tariffs. A tariff is a tax on imports, paid at the border by the company bringing the goods in. Some of that cost gets absorbed. Some gets passed on to you. How much is still debated. In February, the Supreme Court ruled that one law did not give the president the power to set the biggest round of tariffs. Refunds are going to the importers who paid, not straight to you. But other tariffs are still in place. And this week, the White House said it is "just getting started." Supporters say tariffs bring factories home and protect supply chains. Critics say shoppers end up paying. Either way, the next president decides whether those taxes on imports stay, grow, or go.

Door number two. Interest rates. The Federal Reserve kept its key rate between three and three quarters and four percent last month. Two members voted to raise it. The next decision comes December ninth. That rate flows into your mortgage, your car loan, and your credit card. Right now, a thirty year mortgage averages about seven point two percent. A year ago, it was about six point two. On a three hundred thousand dollar loan, that's roughly one hundred ninety dollars more, every single month. There is one upside. Higher rates also mean your savings account can pay you more. The president doesn't set that rate. But the president nominates who sits on the Fed, and the Senate confirms them. And that choice lasts for years.

Door number three. The debt. The federal government owes about forty point six trillion dollars. The legal borrowing limit is about forty one point one. That's roughly half a trillion dollars of room. When it runs out, Congress has to vote again, or the country risks missing payments. And there's a bigger clock. The trustees say Social Security's retirement fund runs out of reserves in late 2032. That's inside the next president's term. Checks don't stop. But without a fix, the money coming in covers only part of what's promised. The fixes on the table are all hard. Raise taxes, trim benefits, or some mix of both. One side leans one way, the other side leans the other. And only Congress can pass it.

So who holds the keys? Think of three. The first key is the White House. It can push tariffs, sign or veto bills, and nominate Fed leaders. The second key is Congress. It controls spending, the debt limit, and Social Security. The third key is the Federal Reserve. It sets rates, and it's built to be independent. No president holds all three. That's why the same problems can outlast any one person in office.

So who feels this? Meet Jake. He's twenty six, he rents, and he wants to buy his first home. Illustrative, but real math. Every month rates stay high, his dream house costs more. His grocery list didn't change. His total did. Then there's his cousin Dana. She's sixty, and she's counting the years to retirement. For Dana, 2032 isn't a headline. It's a plan. She wants to know what her check will look like, and nobody can tell her yet.

And here's the calendar. December ninth: the Fed decides on rates. December eleventh: government funding runs out unless Congress acts. Then the new Congress starts in January. And on November seventh, 2028, Americans vote. Every one of those dates touches your wallet before the next president even moves in.

So what can you do now? Three things. One. Know your rate. If you carry a credit card balance, call and ask for a lower one. It costs nothing to ask. Two. Check your receipts. Track what you spend on groceries for one month, so you see inflation in your own numbers, not just on the news. Three. Look at your Social Security statement at ssa dot gov. It shows what you've earned so far, and it takes ten minutes.

Nobody knows who wins in 2028. But the bills are already written. Prices, rates, and debt don't care who's in the White House. They just get passed down. And the person who pays them, in the end, is you.
